01
Why the current system needs this

Classification drives COA requirements today and will drive legalization & regulation requirements next. The keyword engine was the best possible pre-AI answer, and merchants name products however they like — we can't control that. Four structural problems showed up when we replayed the engine over Westtown's real book:

Nearly half of Westtown's products aren't really classified at all

"Not Specified" fallbackCarrying a real classificationRules replay finds a category for these today

Stored classifications are stale, not wrong: products were classified once at scan time and never re-run as the keyword rules matured. The engine already knows the answer for 95% of the "Not Specified" pile — nothing has asked it since.

The confidence paradox

A product titled just "Lemonade" exactly matches a beverage keyword. The match is perfect — the information isn't. The title alone cannot say whether this is a hemp-infused drink that needs a COA or a plain drink that doesn't. Today the system treats that match with the same certainty as "Delta-8 Pre-Roll 1g." There is no notion of confidence anywhere.

Generic buckets punt the real question

Conditional categories (Beverages-style) defer the COA decision to a global keyword pool with no product context. In the calibrated sample, products landing in a conditional bucket without substance evidence got the COA call right only ~50% of the time — a coin flip. That's the exact population confidence scoring routes to deeper checks.

First-win shadowing

Priority order is first-match-wins: a broad bucket high in the list silently swallows more specific ones below it. Measured: the single keyword thca inside Flower's list steals THCA vapes, pre-rolls, diamonds and seeds — about ⅓ of all high-confidence category errors trace to this one cluster.

Two engines, two answers

The classifier is forked: the Admin API matches title only; the scanner matches name + breadcrumbs/meta, with different defaults. The same product can classify differently depending on which code path touched it last. Unifying this is step zero — no scoring work is trustworthy on a forked engine.

02
The design — four tiers, one engine

Deterministic rules stay in charge. Every product gets a confidence score computed from the match itself. High-confidence products are done — title only, no description risk, no AI cost. Everything else escalates one tier at a time. Manual overrides sit above all of it, untouched.

Tier 1 — Deterministic rules + confidence score title + scraped meta
Today's keyword engine, unified across both repos, evaluating all categories instead of stopping at the first hit. Confidence computed from keyword specificity, bucket generality, competing matches, exclusion proximity, substance evidence, and override history.
▸ 4,112 of 5,682 auto-accepted here (72.4%)
▼ low confidence only
Tier 2 — Description, gated only when Tier 1 can't decide
Same rules re-run over title + description. Because it only runs on products the title already failed, it can rescue a "Lemonade" but can never corrupt a confident title match — which is exactly the always-on misclassification risk that kept description out of the engine until now.
▸ +405 more resolved (79.5% cumulative)
▼ still unresolved
Tier 3 — AI adjudication the tail only
Batched LLM call against a closed taxonomy (our categories + product families — it cannot invent buckets), with title + description + page metadata, required to quote its evidence. Results stamped as AI-sourced, still AUTO — never touches a manual override.
▸ 1,165 products (20.5%) — ≈ $0.58 for all of Westtown
▼ AI uncertain or contradictory
Tier 4 — Human review queue
The existing review modal + weekly export loop, now fed by a "needs review" confidence filter instead of gut feel. Human decisions become MANUAL locks and future training signal.

What actually goes into the confidence score

Every factor is computable from data the platform already has. Nothing here is a vibe — each one moved measured precision during calibration.

Two scores come out, deliberately separate: classification confidence (is the category right?) and COA-requirement confidence (is required/not-required right?). "Delta-9 Lemonade" can be fuzzy on category and near-certain on requirement — and the requirement is what compliance runs on.

"Delta-9 THC Lemonade 12-pack"
Same keyword — but substance evidence fires → conditional cap lifted → HIGH, COA required. Never needs the description at all.
"Mushroom Gummies 500mg"
Two categories match on different keywords (substance axis vs form axis colliding on one list) → competing-match penalty → MEDIUM → resolved by description or AI today, and by the product-family axis permanently (section 06).

04
Calibration — measured, not assumed

Method: the full production matcher was re-implemented faithfully and replayed over Westtown's book (5,682 active products, 54 merchants, 91% with descriptions). A stratified sample of 500 products was blind-labeled — labelers saw only title + description, never the engine's answer — and every claim below is the engine measured against those labels.

The decisive finding: COA-decision precision is flat at ~97–98% from T=0.60 upward — raising the bar past 0.60 doubles the AI tail (20.5% → 41%+) while buying nothing on the compliance decision. T=0.60 auto-accepts 79.5% of the corpus at 97.8% COA precision.

Category precision at the operating point is 83.5% — but most misses are adjacent-form disagreements between two COA-required buckets, which is why the COA decision still lands at 97.8%. The thca cluster is one keyword edit away from fixed; this is exactly the weekly keyword-surgery loop, now fed by measured error clusters instead of complaints. We also tested auto-ranking the winner by keyword specificity instead of priority: it moved more products to high confidence but diluted precision (86% → 84%) — so it's rejected, and the honest fix stays keyword surgery. Negative results published on purpose.

05
Cost — the gate is the cost control, not the model

Because ~80% of products never reach AI, the tail is small enough that model choice barely matters. Prices are current Claude API list prices with the 50% batch discount; a Tier-3 call is ~500 input + ~100 output tokens with the taxonomy prompt cached.

Ongoing cost after backfill: only new and renamed products flow through the tiers — cents per day. Even a worst-case "run 100% of the platform through AI" scenario is ≈ $31 on Haiku. The design's real cost control is the confidence gate keeping 4 of 5 products deterministic.

06
The product-family dependency — how "beverages + conditional" dies

The endgame isn't "MCPD Beverages" as one more keyword bucket next to "Mushroom Beverages" and thirty others. It's two axes: form (what today's classifications already are — beverage, gummy, vape, pre-roll) stays generic on purpose, and family (the substance/regulatory regime — hemp-THC, CBD, mushroom, kava, nicotine, kratom, peptide) becomes its own dimension. "Cannabis beverage" is then a derived display value, not a maintained keyword list.

What family unlocks

  • Conditional COA disappears. Requirement resolves from a small family × form matrix (hemp-THC × any ingestible → required; none × beverage → not required) instead of a global keyword pool.
  • Industry-agnostic for real. Onboarding kava or nicotine = adding a family row + lexicon + matrix rows. No category redesign, ever.
  • Regulation layer. Legalization/regulation requirements key off family × jurisdiction — the second thing classifications must drive.
  • Self-healing. COA-driven promotion: when a lab report later shows Δ9 > 0.3%, the product's family upgrades automatically. The "Lemonade" whose COA arrives corrects itself.

Family signal is already in the data

Blind-label family distribution, Westtown sample (n=500)

Labelers assigned a family confidently for 96% of products — the axis is recoverable from existing text. Tier-3 AI emits a family from day one, so family data accumulates before the axis formally ships.

Stated dependency: Tiers 1–3 (confidence, gated description, AI tail) do not wait for product family — they ship against today's single axis and deliver the precision win now. What does wait for family is retiring generic-conditional buckets and the regulation layer. Family tables are already specified in the MCC/family engineering spec; this proposal consumes them, it doesn't redefine them.

08
Methodology & honest caveats

  • Corpus: 5,682 active Westtown products (54 merchants) from the local platform snapshot; 91% carry descriptions (platform-wide: 96% of 62,479 products).
  • Engine: faithful re-implementation of the production matcher — priority-ascending first-win, word-boundary keyword regex, exclusion veto, conditional keyword pool — extended with all-match tracing and the confidence score.
  • Ground truth: 500-product stratified sample, blind-labeled (title + description only, never the engine's answer) by ten independent labeling passes; average labeler self-confidence 0.90. Category boundaries are genuinely fuzzy in places (a "THCA Diamond Infused Pre-Roll" is legitimately two things), which puts a ceiling on measurable category precision — but not on COA precision.
  • Cleaned before measuring: the snapshot's rule set contained dev/test categories and duplicate buckets, which were removed/merged; the snapshot's manual-override rows turned out to be QA noise and were excluded — the override-history factor therefore runs neutral here and gets its real signal from production's clean audit trail.
  • What production adds (deliberately still wanted): current keyword rules (they're refined weekly; the snapshot's are ~6 weeks stale), merchants onboarded since, and real manual-override history. The description-inclusive production SQL is written and ready — re-running this same harness on that extract is the final seal on the thresholds before anything flips on. If they hold across both datasets, that's stronger evidence than either alone.
  • Precision target is a dial, not a law: T=0.60 is recommended (97.8% COA precision, 20.5% tail). If we want ≥99%, the sweep says the tail grows to ~40% and roughly doubles the (still tiny) AI cost — a product decision, made visible.
