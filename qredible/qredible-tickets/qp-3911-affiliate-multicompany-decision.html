Affiliate Signup Link + Multi-Company-per-User — the strategic call we need to make together

Why you’re reading this: Two linked future capabilities are decision-blocked. One is acquisition — a public /join/{affiliate} signup link so partners can refer merchants without an invite, including a “claim your profile” path when the merchant already exists. The other is commercial — one user managing multiple companies, with subscription tiers gated on how many. Today’s platform supports multi-company, but every user has a single subscription with no limiting factors, so we cannot yet tier on company count. This page lays out today’s state, the three commercial calls, and a recommended default so the team can decide together.

Public signup today
Doesn’t exist. Invites only — every merchant arrives via We Monitor Invites or admin add.
Multi-company today

Commercial calls open
Three. Affiliate free-vs-paid, subscription tiering factor, claim-profile match rule.

The three decisions on the table

D1 — Affiliate access: is the /join/{affiliate} link free, paid, or partner-configurable?
D2 — Tiering factor: what does the subscription gate on — companies per account, products per company, or both?
D3 — Claim-profile rule: how do we reconcile when a signup matches an existing company on DBA / legal name / address / email?

1. How signup & subscriptions work today

An honest baseline of what works, what doesn’t, and the single technical constraint that’s blocking tiering.

Current reality

What works today

  • Multi-company per user: A single user can already be linked to N companies and switch between them.
  • Invite-based onboarding: Processors send invites from We Monitor Invites; new users land in the platform under a chosen company.
  • Partner-code attribution (QP-3898): Each partner has a code; revenue routes through it. Ready to receive affiliate-link traffic.
  • Subscription billing: Plans, paywall, and the plan catalog are in place — usable for paid signups.
  • Duplicate signals exist: DBA names, legal name, locations/addresses, and email domain are all captured — the data needed for a match is there.

What’s blocking us

  • No public signup URL: The platform has no /join/{affiliate} route. Every signup today is invite-mediated.
  • One subscription per user, regardless of companies: A user with 3 companies pays the same as a user with 1. There is no per-company-count metering and no tier limit to compare against.
  • No company-count limiting factor on plans: Plans have feature toggles but no numeric cap on companies. The same gap QP-3876 closes for product count.
  • No claim-profile flow: If a new signup matches an existing company on DBA / address / email, there is no reconciliation UI. The user either creates a duplicate or gets an error.
  • No affiliate routing rules: Even if a partner had a link, the platform has nothing to read the affiliate slug, attribute the visit, or scope the plan picker.
How signup works under the hood today: A processor (or admin) creates an invite tied to a company in We Monitor Invites; the merchant clicks the link, lands on the platform, and is added as a user of that company. Subscription is created at the user level, not the company level, and carries no “max companies” ceiling. Switching companies is just a context toggle — nothing is metered.
Subscription = pl_subscription ↔ user. Company memberships = pl_company_user (many-to-many, no count constraint). No /join route exists; no affiliate slug is read; no claim-match table exists.
Why we can’t tier today: A tier (“up to 3 companies”, “up to 25 products”) requires (a) a numeric cap field on the plan, (b) server-side enforcement on every path that creates the metered thing, and (c) visible usage so the user knows when they’re hitting the limit. None of these exist for company count. QP-3876 is building this primitive for product count; the same machinery would extend to company count, but it has to be designed in.

3. The three commercial decisions we need to make

Each one is a yes/no that shapes the build. Picking the wrong default is reversible but expensive after launch.

Decisions needed
D1

Affiliate access — free, paid, or partner-configurable?

When a merchant lands via /join/{affiliate}, does the platform let them in for free (and monetize on growth), force a paid plan up front, or let the partner choose per deal?

A · Free entry Lowest funnel friction. Merchant lands, sets up a company, hits limits, upgrades. Partner attributes revenue at the upgrade event. Cheaper for partners to send traffic — but free infra cost on us, and revenue attribution lags.
B · Paid up front Plan picker at signup, partner code applies, revenue is immediate & cleanly attributed. Cleaner books, but partners may resist sending traffic to a paywall and conversion drops.
C · Partner-configurable Each affiliate slug has its own setting: FlexPay paid, generic referral free, internal team free. Most flexibility, but moves the choice (and the support cost) onto every partner deal.
D2

Subscription tiering — what does the plan gate on?

If we want plans to scale with usage, what’s the metered unit? Companies-per-account, products-per-company, or both? This determines the data model we add to pl_plan and which paths need enforcement.

A · Companies per account Free = 1 company, Growth = up to 5, Enterprise = unlimited. Matches the multi-company use case head-on; clean upgrade trigger. Doesn’t cap a user with 1 huge company.
B · Products per company Already being designed for QP-3876. Caps catalog size per company but lets a user own unlimited shell companies. Easier to ship today — doesn’t require new primitives.
C · Both (compound) Plans carry both caps. Most accurate metering, scales with both axes of value. Highest build effort and more nuanced upgrade prompts to design.
D3

Claim-profile reconciliation — how strict is the match?

When the affiliate signup form submits a merchant that might already be in our system (entered under a different DBA, address, or email by a processor), what do we do? Auto-match? Show candidates? Require exact match? Wrong call here either creates duplicates or leaks processor-collected data.

A · Strict exact match Match only on legal name + address (or EIN if collected). Lowest false-positive; highest false-negative → the user creates a duplicate. Long-term, we end up with many duplicate companies to merge.
B · Fuzzy candidates → user confirms Run a multi-signal match (DBA, address, email domain, phone). Show 0-5 likely candidates. User picks “this is me, claim it” or “none, create new.” Balanced; requires a candidate-display UI and an ops review queue for sensitive claims.
C · Auto-claim on multi-signal If 3+ signals match (email + DBA + zip), auto-attach the new user to the existing company. Lowest friction; highest risk — a wrong claim hands a stranger access to a processor’s collected scans & COAs.
The three decisions are coupled. A free affiliate (D1·A) only makes sense if there’s a tier the merchant can actually hit (D2 — ideally B or C). A claim-profile that hands over processor data (D3·C) is a much bigger risk if affiliate access is free and unauthenticated. Pick them as a set, not in isolation.

4. The options, side-by-side

For each axis, what each option ships, what it costs to build, and the trade-offs.

Options matrix
D1

Affiliate access model

A · Free entry, monetize on growth

Land → set up → hit a limit → upgrade. Partner attribution fires at the upgrade event.

  • +Lowest top-of-funnel friction
  • +Lets the product sell itself
  • +Partners love sending free-trial traffic
  • ~Requires tiers that bite (D2)
  • −Revenue lag; free-tier infra cost
B · Paid up front, plan picker at signup

Plan picker on the affiliate landing page; partner code routes attribution; merchant pays before landing in-product.

  • +Immediate, cleanly-attributed revenue
  • +Self-selects committed merchants
  • +Simplest commercial story
  • ~Higher friction — conversion drops
  • ~Partners may resist paywalled links
C · Partner-configurable per slug

Each affiliate has a free/paid setting (and could even pin a starting plan). FlexPay paid, ISO trials free.

  • +Maximum flexibility per deal
  • +A/B-able by partner
  • −Most build effort (config UI + routing)
  • −Operational complexity per partner
  • ~Easy to add later on top of A or B
D2

Multi-company subscription structure

A · Cap companies per account

Plan has a max_companies field. Free = 1, Growth = 5, Enterprise = unlimited. Enforced on add-company.

  • +Matches the multi-company use case
  • +Clear upgrade trigger
  • +Single new primitive to design
  • ~Doesn’t scale revenue with catalog size
  • ~1-company power users pay flat
B · Cap products per company (only)

Use QP-3876’s product-count limit as the sole metering. Unlimited companies; each company carries its own product cap.

  • +Ships fastest — primitive already in flight
  • +Caps the high-cost axis (scans, COAs)
  • −Power users own N shell companies for free
  • −Misses the multi-company use case entirely
C · Compound — both caps

Plans carry both max_companies and max_products_per_company. Tier = both axes scale together.

  • +Meters both axes of customer value
  • +Reuses QP-3876’s limiting-factor primitive
  • +Most defensible pricing story
  • ~Two enforcement points to maintain
  • ~Upgrade UX needs both axes shown
D3

Claim-profile reconciliation

A · Strict exact match

Match only on legal name + address (or EIN). Anything else → create new company.

  • +Lowest false-positive risk
  • +Minimal build — pure equality check
  • −High duplicate rate (we’ll merge later)
  • −Misses obvious matches via DBA/email
B · Fuzzy candidates → user confirms

Multi-signal scoring (DBA, address, email domain, phone). Show 0-5 candidates. User picks one or “none.” Sensitive claims (cross-processor data) gated by ops review.

  • +Balanced friction vs. accuracy
  • +User stays in control of claim decision
  • +Ops review queue gates risky claims
  • ~Needs candidate-list UI + scoring config
  • ~Match rules tunable, not hard-coded
C · Auto-claim on multi-signal

If ≥3 signals agree (email + DBA + zip), attach the new user to the existing company silently.

  • +Lowest user friction by far
  • −Data-leak risk — a wrong claim hands a stranger processor scans & COAs
  • −Hard to undo cleanly
  • −Conflicts with policy-first auditability

The single biggest dependency: the plan-limiting-factor primitive itself. QP-3876 is building it for product count. If we extend that same primitive to cover company count (adding max_companies alongside max_products), then D2 is mostly “define the tiers and wire the enforcement points” rather than “invent new architecture.” If we ship product-count limits without considering this extension, we’ll rebuild it later.

Recommended default — for discussion, not decided

If we had to pick today, the combo below balances revenue clarity, top-of-funnel friction, build cost, and reversibility. Each pick is independently revisable, and we can ship the affiliate route on top of any D2/D3 combination — so D1 doesn’t have to wait on the others.

D1 · Default
B — Paid up front

Plan picker at signup. Cleanest revenue attribution via partner code. Add D1·C (partner-configurable free/paid) later if a deal demands it.

D2 · Default
C — Compound caps

Plans gate on both companies-per-account and products-per-company. Reuses QP-3876’s primitive; meters both axes of customer value.

D3 · Default
B — Fuzzy + confirm

Multi-signal candidates, user confirms, ops review for cross-processor claims. Avoids both the data-leak risk of auto-claim and the duplicate spam of strict-match.

Net effect: a partner sends /join/flexpay, the merchant picks a plan, the signup form runs a match and shows candidates (or creates new), and the resulting subscription carries the FlexPay partner code. A power user can upgrade to add a 2nd company; the same plan caps their per-company catalog. Nothing in this combo conflicts with QP-3876 or QP-3898.

Why this combo

  • Revenue clarity: paid-up-front + partner code = an attributed transaction every time.
  • Build economy: reuses QP-3876’s primitive instead of inventing a parallel one.
  • Lowest data-risk: human-confirmed claim with ops review for the dangerous cases.
  • Reversible: we can flip D1 to free per-partner later without touching D2 or D3.
  • Policy-first compatible: claim decisions become versioned scoring config + audit trail, not hard-coded rules.

6. How to evaluate any alternative the team proposes

If the default doesn’t fit, score the alternative against these five lenses. The combo that wins on the most lenses wins.

Evaluation framework

1Revenue clarity

Can a finance person look at a transaction and say which partner earned it, and which plan triggered it? Paid-up-front and explicit caps both score high here; free-trial with delayed upgrade scores lower.

2Top-of-funnel friction

Would a partner happily send their merchants through this link? Every form field and paywall step costs conversion. Use the partner’s point of view as the tiebreaker.

3Build economy

Does this reuse a primitive already on the roadmap (QP-3876, QP-3898, QP-3906), or does it invent a parallel system? Parallel systems double the maintenance bill.

4Data-risk & compliance fit

Does this expose processor-collected data to the wrong user? Does it preserve the policy-first audit story (versioned config, not hard-coded rules)? Auto-claim fails here; human-confirm passes.

5Reversibility

If we’re wrong about any of D1/D2/D3, how expensive is it to change later? Prefer additive moves (start strict, loosen later) over destructive ones (start permissive, tighten later).

6Compatibility with existing tickets

Does the pick stay aligned with QP-3898 (partner code attribution), QP-3876 (plan limiting factors), and QP-3906 (DBA ↔ location)? If we drift, those tickets have to absorb the cost.
