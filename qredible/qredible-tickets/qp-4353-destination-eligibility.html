State-level shipping restrictions — the model and the merchant surface

Adds no new engine concepts: one new axis (jurisdiction) on the policy model from QP-4352, and one new surface (the destination matrix) built from the popover pattern already signed off there.

Q3 — Where does state authority fit in the policy model?

The same three-layer pattern, applied on a second axis. A state policy is a policy row like any other (QP-4352 already names "a state regime" as valid). The one difference: applicability resolves by jurisdiction, not by processor selection — a state's policy applies whenever that state is the evaluated destination (or, for a handful of regimes, the origin), regardless of processor preference. A processor cannot opt out of a state policy, mirroring the federal asymmetry rule. Overlays on state policies are restriction-only: narrow yes, widen never — and a widening attempt is refused at write time, when the person configuring can still see the error.

Origin matters, not just destination. Eligibility is a function of (product, origin, destination). A merchant with multiple fulfillment sites gets a verdict per site. This is carried in the evaluation signature from day one because destination-only is very hard to retrofit.

Q4 — What does a merchant see? The Destination Matrix

One cell per jurisdiction on the product's Shipping tab, showing the effective destination verdict. The map is the summary; the popover is the truth — clicking a cell opens the same authority-breakdown popover from QP-4352, arithmetic included. No verdict may appear on the map that cannot be expanded into its arithmetic.

What state policies must be able to express

Fourteen predicate shapes; the regulatory team owns the values. Concentration, mg/serving, mg/container, compound prohibition (with de minimis floors), synthetic/derivation, ratios, product form, servings caps — plus the ones that break a COA-only model: registration requirements, testing provenance, labeling/packaging, age gates, channel prohibitions, and carriers modeled as policies (UPS/FedEx/USPS conditions as a CARRIER-tier policy with the mandatory "not a determination of law" qualifier). A product can pass every analyte rule and still be non-shippable because it is unregistered — which is why non-COA predicates are a sibling family beside the analyte evaluator, not a bolt-on.

The one genuine data gap: Product Presentation. Per-serving and per-container predicates need net weight, serving size, servings per container, product form and packaging attributes per SKU/batch — with provenance and confidence per field. A low-confidence presentation field yields INSUFFICIENT_DATA for anything that depends on it, never a computed verdict. Ships as its own ticket ahead of the matrix; extraction sits with the scan pipeline.
Determinations produced by this system are compliance tooling based on published rules and merchant-supplied data. They are not legal advice, are not a determination that a shipment is lawful, and do not substitute for the merchant's own legal review or its carrier's acceptance policies.

Answers to the addendum's open questions (condensed)

  • Evaluation cost at 50+ jurisdictions: fine — the expensive work (corpus resolution, normalization, reconciliation) is jurisdiction-invariant and computed once; per-state fan-out is arithmetic. Snapshots go lazy/incremental, keyed to policy version + input hash + jurisdiction, so storage tracks policy and COA churn, not the state count.
  • Where Product Presentation lives: a separate versioned object tied to batch (batch-level truth + per-field provenance/confidence demand it); the evaluator already consumes its fields as parameters, so only a resolver is needed.
  • Overlay directionality: did not exist — now built (Q3 row above).
  • Ship-from: was not modeled as evaluation input — the seam now exists (C2); company-profile addresses give the data a home when the feature builds.
  • Storage at 50×: viable with the incremental keying above; most states agree on most products and can share canonical rows.
